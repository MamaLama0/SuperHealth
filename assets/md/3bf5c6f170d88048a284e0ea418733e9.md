While heavily training the lats and teres major through their shoulder functions, every vertical pull will also train the primary elbow flexor muscles, namely the biceps, brachialis, and brachioradialis. With the notable elbow flexion demands imposed by vertical pulls, it is clear that the primary elbow flexor muscles will meaningfully function during these exercises. However, how effectively the elbow flexor muscles are trained by a vertical pull in comparison to a single joint curl variation is not as certain. Gentil et al. (2013) provided insight into this matter by assigning 29 untrained participants to one of two 10-week training programs and measuring elbow flexor muscle thickness (which would reflect the biceps and the brachialis, which lies deep to the biceps) with ultrasound before and after the interventions. Both groups trained twice per week with 3 sets of 8-12 reps for each exercise, and both programs involved the bench press and lat pulldown. One group also included biceps curls and triceps extensions (with the specific variations not mentioned by the authors) performed alongside the compound exercises to determine how the addition of curls may influence changes in elbow flexor muscle size and strength. Following the training period, the two groups experienced similar average increases in elbow flexor muscle thickness, as demonstrated by the compound exercise-only group having a 6.5% increase and the group with the curls having a 7.0% increase. Max elbow flexion strength gains (measured with an isokinetic dynamometer) were also similar, with the compound exercise-only group having a 10.4% increase and the group with the curls having a 12.9% increase.

In a later study with a similar design, Gentil et al. (2015) also assessed 29 untrained participants performing one of two 10-week programs with two sessions per week, where each exercise was trained for 3 sets of 8-12 reps. Both groups trained a bench press, leg curl, and leg press, but one group’s program also contained a barbell biceps curl, while the other group’s program contained a wide overhand grip lat pulldown. After completion of the programs, the curl group experienced a 5.8% increase in average elbow flexor muscle thickness, and the pulldown group similarly had a 6.1% increase. Strength gains followed the same trend, as indicated by the curl group and pulldown group respectively experiencing 11.9% and 10.4% increases in average elbow flexion strength.

We can anticipate that new research on this topic will emerge in the future, but at the time this passage was written in the summer of 2026, these are the only two studies that have directly investigated how vertical pulls may compare to curls for stimulating increases in elbow flexor muscle size. While their findings are notable, they do not necessarily settle the question of how well vertical pulls may train the elbow flexors compared to curls.

When thinking about how generalizable this data may be, it is worth noting that Gentil et al. (2015) specifically assessed wide overhand grip lat pulldowns. Gentil et al. (2013) may not have explicitly specified the grip used, but it is safe to assume that their participants also used an overhand grip, since that is essentially the default grip for pulldowns. With the same authors conducting both studies, the earlier study may have used a wide grip as well, but we do not know with any degree of confidence. The biceps has better leverage for flexing the elbow with an underhand grip compared to an overhand grip, and its EMG activity has been measured to be greater with an underhand grip biceps curl than an overhand grip curl. In contrast, the brachialis, which was also included in the elbow flexor muscle measurements by the two studies from Gentil et al., has similar leverage for flexing the elbow regardless of forearm position. Correspondingly, an overhand grip vertical pull would not be expected to train the elbow flexor muscles overall any more effectively than a neutral or underhand grip vertical pull. Some data indicate that a wide overhand grip may, on average, involve slightly less biceps EMG activity during a vertical pull than other grips, but the difference is of an insignificant degree. While the potential exists for the specific grip to influence how well the biceps are trained during a vertical pull, there is no reason to think that closer grips, underhand grips, or neutral grips would train the biceps any less effectively than the wide overhand grip pull that was studied by Gentil et al. (2015).

When considering how the findings from Gentil et al. may apply to other situations, the training status of their participants is another noteworthy variable. Vertical pulls being just as effective as biceps curls for stimulating elbow flexor muscle growth in untrained individuals does not necessarily preclude a difference from existing in individuals with training experience. Perhaps an exercise that trains a muscle group moderately well may produce similar results to an exercise training the muscle group very well in untrained individuals, but the difference may become evident in trained individuals. However, other studies on untrained individuals have been able to find significant differences in muscle growth when comparing compound and single joint exercises. For instance, in untrained individuals, biceps curls have been found to induce greater elbow flexor growth than dumbbell rows, and skullcrushes have been found to induce greater total triceps growth than bench presses.
